Martin Heidegger is without doubt the most incomprehensible German
philosopher who ever lived. Nothing quite rivals the prose of his
masterpiece, Being and Time, which is filled with complex German compound
words such as ‘Seinsvergessenheit’, ‘Bodenständigkeit’ and
‘Wesensverfassung’! Yet beneath the jargon, Heidegger tells us some
simple, even at times homespun, truths about the meaning of our lives, the
sicknesses of our time and the routes to freedom.

We should bother with him. He was born and, in many ways, remained a rural,
provincial German. He loved picking mushrooms, walking in the countryside
and going to bed early. He hated television, aeroplanes, pop music and
processed food. At one time, he had been a supporter of Hitler, but later
saw the error of his ways. He spent much of his life in a hut in the woods,
away from modern civilisation. He diagnosed modern humanity as suffering
from a number of diseases of the soul.

First: We have forgotten to notice that we're alive.

We know it in theory, of course, but in our day-to-day lives we aren't
properly in touch with the sheer mystery of existence: the mystery of what
Heidegger called ‘das Sein’, or ‘Being’ in English. It's only at a few odd
moments, perhaps late at night, when we're ill and have been alone all day,
or when we're out walking in the countryside, that we come up against the
uncanny strangeness of everything: why things exist as they do, why we are
here rather than there, and why the world is as it is. What we're running
away from is a confrontation with the opposite of Being, which Heidegger
called ‘das Nichts’ (the Nothing).

The second problem is that we've forgotten that all Being is connected.
Most of the time, our jobs and daily routines make us egoistic and narrowly
focused. We treat others and nature as means rather than ends.
But occasionally — and, once again, walks in the countryside are
particularly conducive to this realisation — we may step outside our narrow
orbit and take a more expansive view. We may sense what Heidegger termed
the ‘Unity of Being’, noticing, for example, that we, the ladybird on the
bark, the rock and the cloud over there all exist right now and are
fundamentally united by the basic fact of our common being. Heidegger
values these moments immensely and wants us to use them as a springboard
for a deeper form of generosity, an overcoming of alienation and egoism,
and a more profound appreciation of the brief time that remains to us
before ‘das Nichts’ (the Nothing) claims us in turn.
The third problem is that we forget how to be free and to live for
ourselves.

Of course, much about us is not free. We are — in Heidegger's unusual
formulation — ‘thrown into the world’ at the start of our lives: thrown
into a particular and narrow social milieu, surrounded by rigid attitudes,
archaic prejudices and practical necessities not of our own making. The
philosopher wants to help us overcome this ‘thrownness’ (‘Geworfenheit’, as
he puts it in German). To understand this, we need to recognise our
psychological, social and professional provincialism, and then rise above
it to gain a more universal perspective. In so doing, we'll make the
classic Heideggerian journey from ‘Uneigentlichkeit’ to ‘Eigentlichkeit’
(from inauthenticity to authenticity). We will, in essence, start to live
for ourselves.

And yet, for Heidegger, most of the time we fail dismally at this task. We
merely surrender to a socialised, superficial mode of being that he called
the ‘they-self’ (as opposed to the ‘our-selves’). We follow the chatter
(‘das Gerede’ in German) that we encounter in newspapers, on television and
in the large cities where Heidegger hated spending time. What will help us
pull away from the ‘they-self’ is an appropriately intense focus on our own
impending death. It's only when we realise that other people cannot save us
from ‘das Nichts’ (the Nothing) that we're likely to stop living for them,
stop worrying so much about what others think, and stop giving up the lion's
share of our lives and energies to impress people who never really liked us
in the first place. During a lecture in 1961, when Heidegger was asked how
we should lead our lives better, he replied tersely that we should simply
aim to spend more time ‘in graveyards’.

It would be a lie to say that Heidegger's meaning and message are ever
entirely clear. Nevertheless, what he tells us is intermittently
fascinating, wise and surprisingly useful. Despite his extraordinary
vocabulary and style, we know much of this already. We merely need to be
reminded and emboldened to take it seriously — and his unusual prose style
may help us do so. We know in our hearts that it is time to overcome our
‘Geworfenheit’ (thrownness), that we should become more conscious of ‘das
Nichts’ (the Nothing) in our day-to-day lives, and that we owe it to
ourselves to escape the clutches of ‘das Gerede’ (the chatter) for the sake
of ‘Eigentlichkeit’ (authenticity) — with a little help from that graveyard.
